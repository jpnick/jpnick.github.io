Policy Paper

The Impact of Transboundary Haze on Lung Cancer Mortality in Thailand

This study investigates the long-term health impacts of exposure to PM2.5 air pollutants on lung cancer mortality. Using data from ground-based stations, Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD), and estimated PM2.5 levels, we found a significant correlation between long-term PM2.5 exposure and increased lung cancer mortality.

Specifically, over a 10-year period, especially during haze seasons, each one microgram per cubic meter (µg/m³) increase in PM2.5 concentration is associated with a 0.275 percentage point rise in lung cancer mortality probability.

While no conclusive evidence was found linking PM2.5 to lung cancer mortality over shorter exposure periods, such as five years, the long-term impacts underscore the urgent need for effective air quality management policies.

Keywords: Lung Cancer Mortality, Southeast Asia, Transboundary Haze, Air Quality, Particulate Matter, PM2.5, AOD

Spatial Distribution & Data Gaps

Mapping the concentration of pollutants reveals stark regional disparities and highlights the critical limitations of ground-level monitoring.

Limitations of Current Data

  • Ground stations have severely limited coverage (only 61 stations, covering 6.6% of 928 districts). Massive blind spots remain.
  • Many ground stations possess a maximum historical data period of only 10 years, restricting long-term studies.
  • Lung cancer mortality at the highly granular district level is available exclusively in Thailand within the studied region.

The Core Research Question

"Is there a connection between lung cancer and the effects of transboundary haze in northern Southeast Asia?"

Methodology & Satellite Data

To overcome ground station limitations, this research utilizes satellite remote sensing to estimate air quality across all districts.

Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD)

Data retrieved from NASA's MODIS satellite, capturing atmospheric transparency and particles globally.

100% District Coverage

Unlike ground stations (6.6%), satellite AOD covers all 928 districts in Thailand, resolving spatial limitations.

20-Year Historical Data

Access to data from 2001 to 2021, crucial for observing long-term health impacts like cancer development.

Key Findings & Discussion

0.275%
Rise in Mortality Probability

Associated with every 1 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5 concentration over a 10-year period, specifically driven by exposure during the Jan-Apr haze seasons.

10 Years
Minimum Exposure Required

No conclusive evidence was found linking PM2.5 to lung cancer mortality over shorter periods (e.g., 5 years), underscoring the long-term compounding danger of seasonal haze.

Policy Implications

The alignment between AOD-based PM2.5 estimates and ground-based measurements supports these findings. Policymakers must act on the severe health risks associated with long-term exposure:

Enhance air quality monitoring and regulation beyond sparse ground stations.
Implement targeted health interventions for vulnerable populations in northern regions.
Launch aggressive public awareness campaigns before and during the Jan-Apr haze cycle.
Support funding for longitudinal studies exploring varied exposure timeframes.
